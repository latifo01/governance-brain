# AI societal impact and stakeholder engagement

## Summary

Societal impact governance examines how an AI system affects people, groups,
communities, organisations and the environment in its actual context of use.
Technical evaluation can estimate performance, but sustained engagement and
contextual evidence are needed to understand who is affected, how impacts are
experienced and whether the system remains fit for purpose.

## Applicability

This note applies when an AI system can affect people beyond its development
team, including users, customers, workers, communities, downstream recipients
or the environment. The depth and form of engagement should reflect purpose,
impact, risk tolerance, affected populations and available evidence. NIST AI RMF
Playbook guidance is voluntary and should be combined with applicable law,
sector rules and binding impact-assessment requirements where relevant.

## Governance considerations

- Identify intended and unanticipated uses, affected individuals and groups,
  social and cultural context, downstream dependencies and potential positive
  and negative impacts before deployment.
- Establish a repeatable way to collect, evaluate, prioritise and incorporate
  feedback from users, domain experts and potentially affected communities.
  Engagement should begin early, continue through the lifecycle and have a
  stated purpose so that consultation does not become a merely symbolic step.
- Record which feedback was received, how it was assessed, what changed as a
  result, and why a concern was accepted, mitigated, escalated or left open.
  Provide appropriate routes for reporting problems, appealing outcomes or
  raising concerns about bias, accessibility or harm.
- Use impact, likelihood and organisational risk tolerance to prioritise
  resources and determine approval, conditional approval, redesign, restricted
  use or decommissioning decisions. Revisit these decisions when monitoring or
  stakeholder evidence changes.
- Where material, include environmental indicators such as energy, water and
  emissions, and record the baseline, trade-offs and actions associated with
  significant changes.

## Additional evidence-qualified controls

- Define an environmental measurement boundary and baseline where resource use may be material, and record the data quality, assumptions and materiality rationale.
- Select indicators and a review cadence proportionate to the system, its lifecycle and affected groups; retain the evidence used for significant trade-offs.
- Connect material impact findings and stakeholder feedback to an accountable decision path for mitigation, restricted use, redesign or decommissioning.

The reviewed framework supports this governance pattern but does not prescribe universal environmental metrics, thresholds or reporting duties.

## Limits

Stakeholder engagement is evidence about context and experience; it is not by
itself a legal conclusion, a representative statistical sample or proof that a
system is safe. Participation can miss people who cannot access the process,
and technical metrics can miss harms that are not measured. The source does not
define one mandatory method, threshold or stakeholder composition. Binding
rights, privacy, discrimination, safety and environmental requirements require
separate authority-specific analysis.
