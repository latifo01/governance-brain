# Executive Summary

**SR 11-7** (*Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management*), issued jointly by the Federal Reserve Board (FRB) and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) in 2011, serves as the foundational regulatory standard for managing model risk in banking organizations. 

Rather than replacing SR 11-7 for artificial intelligence, the Federal Reserve maintains that **SR 11-7 is technology-agnostic and principles-based**, making it fully applicable to traditional quantitative models, advanced ML algorithms, and Generative AI (GenAI). However, supervisory focus emphasizes that AI/ML models introduce unique complexities—such as opacity ("black box" design), dynamic learning, non-deterministic outputs, and heavy reliance on third-party vendors—requiring financial institutions to adapt their Model Risk Management (MRM) frameworks to address these heightened risks.

---

# 1. What is SR 11-7?

SR 11-7 establishes expectations for managing the risks associated with using quantitative models in financial decision-making.

### Definition of a "Model" under SR 11-7
The guidance defines a model as a quantitative method, system, or approach that applies statistical, economic, financial, or mathematical theories, techniques, and assumptions to process input data into quantitative outputs (estimates, forecasts, classifications, or scores).

### The Three Core Pillars of SR 11-7
1. **Model Development, Implementation, and Use:** Requires clear statement of purpose, rigorous design, robust data quality standards, conceptual soundness, and testing.
2. **Model Validation:** Independent verification consisting of:
   * **Conceptual Soundness:** Evaluating the theoretical basis and design choices.
   * **Ongoing Monitoring:** Verifying that the model continues to perform as intended over time.
   * **Outcomes Analysis:** Comparing model outputs against actual results (e.g., backtesting).
3. **Governance, Policies, and Controls:** Board and senior management oversight, comprehensive model inventory, clear roles/responsibilities, and audit oversight.

---

# 2. Applicability of SR 11-7 to AI and Machine Learning

AI and ML tools—including neural networks, ensemble models, and Large Language Models (LLMs)—clearly fall under the SR 11-7 definition whenever they process inputs to generate scores, classifications, text-based quantitative summaries, or risk assessments used in business decisions.

While the principles of SR 11-7 remain constant, AI introduces operational and theoretical challenges that alter how MRM teams must execute compliance:

| Challenge Area | Traditional Models | AI / Machine Learning Models | SR 11-7 Impact / Expectation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Explainability** | Linear/Parametric (e.g., Logistic Regression); easily interpretable coefficients. | Complex non-linear relationships, multi-layered neural networks ("black box"). | **Conceptual Soundness:** Institutions must explain *why* an AI model arrives at a decision using explainable AI (XAI) tools (e.g., SHAP, LIME). |
| **Data Dependencies** | Structured, historical numeric data. | High-dimensional, unstructured data (text, images, audio, alternative data). | **Data Integrity:** Strict verification of data bias, data lineage, and representative training datasets. |
| **Model Stability** | Static assumptions updated periodically. | Rapid retraining, continuously updating models, data drift. | **Ongoing Monitoring:** Shift from periodic (annual) monitoring to automated, near-real-time monitoring for drift and performance decay. |
| **Determinism** | Deterministic (same inputs = same outputs). | Probabilistic / Non-deterministic (especially in Generative AI). | **Outcomes Analysis:** Traditional backtesting must be expanded to test output variance, hallucination rates, and prompt stability. |

# 3. FRB Supervisory Focus and Guidance Framework

In alignment with federal directives (e.g., Executive Order 14110 on AI) and interagency standards, the FRB's supervisory expectations regarding AI under SR 11-7 focus on key strategic areas:

### A. Generative AI (GenAI) and LLMs
* **Classification:** The FRB emphasizes that even if a GenAI model generates text or code rather than a numerical credit score, its downstream usage in decision-making (e.g., automated customer interaction, loan processing summarization, code generation for trading algorithms) introduces operational and model risk subject to SR 11-7 principles or related tool/operational risk controls.
* **Risk Vectors:** Examiners look closely at mitigation strategy for **hallucinations**, **prompt injection/jailbreaking**, **intellectual property/copyright liabilities**, and **data leakage**.

### B. Conceptual Soundness & Explainability (XAI)
* Regulators require that "black box" models are not adopted solely due to incremental gains in predictive power if they lack explainability.
* Banks must demonstrate that explainability tools (e.g., feature importance metrics) accurately represent the underlying logic, especially for consumer-facing models subject to Fair Lending laws (ECOA, FCRA) where adverse action notices are required.

### C. Continuous Monitoring & Algorithmic Drift
* Traditional point-in-time model validation is insufficient for AI models operating in dynamic economic environments.
* Examiners expect automated controls to detect **Data Drift** (changes in input data distributions) and **Concept Drift** (changes in the relationship between input data and target variables).

### D. Integration with Third-Party Risk Management (TPRM)
* Many financial institutions deploy third-party or vendor-provided AI models (e.g., cloud-hosted LLMs, vendor credit scoring tools).
* **Interagency Alignment:** SR 11-7 enforcement heavily intersects with the *Interagency Guidance on Third-Party Relationships: Risk Management*. Banks cannot cite vendor proprietary confidentiality as an excuse for avoiding validation; they must request model documentation, conduct independent bench testing, or establish compensating controls.

### E. Convergence with AI Risk Frameworks (e.g., NIST AI RMF)
* Federal Reserve guidance encourages aligning SR 11-7 model risk governance with broader AI governance standard frameworks, such as the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)**, to address non-quantitative risks like ethics, privacy, and systemic bias.

# 4. Key Takeaways for Financial Institutions

To satisfy SR 11-7 compliance for AI/ML models, institutions should ensure the following practices are established:

1. **Expand Model Inventories:** Clearly define and inventory all AI, ML, and GenAI use cases, distinguishing between full models (SR 11-7 compliant) and AI-enabled tools (governed under operational/IT risk frameworks).
2. **Adapt Validation Methodologies:** Develop specialized testing protocols for ML models, including explainability assessments, hyperparameter tuning evaluations, and stress testing under out-of-distribution scenarios.
3. **Establish Cross-Functional Governance:** AI risk management requires collaboration across Model Risk Management (MRM), Information Security, Data Governance, Legal/Compliance (Fair Lending), and Business Units.
4. **Enforce Vendor AI Accountability:** Ensure vendor contracts provide sufficient transparency, audit rights, and validation capabilities for third-party AI systems.
